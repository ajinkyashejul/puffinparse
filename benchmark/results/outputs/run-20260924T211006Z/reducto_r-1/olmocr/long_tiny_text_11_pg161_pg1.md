PLOWEFERE. Companion in play. (<i>A.-S.</i>)
PLOWKKY. Covered with pimples.

For hymne that is smetyne with his awenne blode,
and spredis over alle his lymmes, and waxes <i>plowkky</i>,
and brekes owte. <i>MS. Lincoln Med. f. 294.</i>

PLOW-LODE. “<i>Caracuta</i>, plow lode,” No-
minale MS. It seems to be the same as
<i>Plough-land</i>, q. v.

PLOWMELL. A small wooden hammer occa-
sionally fixed to the plough, still used in the
North; in the Midland counties in its stead is
used a plough-hatchet.

PLOWRING. Weeping. <i>Prompt. Parv.</i>

PLOWSHO. A ploughshare. <i>Kennett.</i>

PLOY. A merry-meeting. <i>North.</i>

PLOYE. A plough. Nominale MS.

PLUCK. (1) Courage. <i>Var. dial.</i> “To pluck
up one’s heart,” to be bold, to rejoice. Against
the pluck, i. e. against the inclination.

	(2) To pluck a crow or goose with any one, i. e.
to quarrel with him.

	(3) To pluck a rose, i. e. to go to the jakes, said
of women. Middleton, iv. 222.

	(4) A dry pluck, i. e. a severe stroke.
This same is kind cuckolds luck :
These fellowes have given me a drie <i>pluck</i>,
Now I have never a crose to blesse me.
<i>Mariage of Witt and Wisdom, 1579.</i>

	(5)
Our kynge and Robyn rode togyder,
Forsoth as I you say,
And they shote <i>plucke</i> buffet,
As they went by the way. <i>Robin Hood, i. 75.</i>

	(6) Same as (1)?
--- I had the luck
To see, and drink a little <i>pluck</i>.
<i>Brome’s Songs, 1661, p. 167.</i>

(7) A student who fails in an university exa-
mination is said to be <i>plucked</i>.

PLUCKING. The worsted plucked from the
machine while the wheel is turning. <i>North.</i>

PLUERE. Weeping. (<i>A.-N.</i>)

PLUF. A tube of tin through which boys blow
peas. <i>Linc.</i> Also called a <i>pluffer</i>.

PLUFE. A plough. <i>Yorksh.</i>

PLUFFY. Spongy; porous. <i>Devon.</i> It is some-
times explained, soft, plump.

PLUG. A dwarfish fellow. <i>East.</i>

PLUM. (1) Light; soft. <i>West.</i>

(2) Sensible; honest. <i>North.</i>

(3) Very; exceedingly. <i>Kent.</i>

(4) Straight; upright; perpendicular. <i>Plum</i>
<i>downe</i>, Cotgrave in v. <i>Escarpé</i>.

(5) <i>Plum round</i>, quite round. “Make their
attire to sit <i>plum round</i>,” Harrison, p. 172.
<i>Plum fat</i>, Florio, p. 33.

PLUMAKIN. The magnum-bonum plum.

PLUME. To pick or pluck the feathers off a
hawk or other bird.

PLUMED-SWAN. A white colour. One of the
terms of ancient alchemy.

PLUMMY. Soft; wet; mouldy. <i>Devon.</i>

PLUMP. (1) Dry; hard. <i>Kent.</i>

(2) A clump of trees. <i>North.</i>

(3) A crowd of people; a mass of anything. It
is sometimes a verb, to collect together.

“Assemble theymselves in plumpes,” More’s
Suppllycacyon of Soulys, sig. F. ii.

Rydes into rowte his dede to revenge,

Presede into the <i>plume</i> and with a prynce mctes.

<i>Morte Arthure, MS. Lincoln, f. 76.</i>

When kynge Richard perceved that the people by
<i>plumpes</i> fled from hym to Duke Henry.

<i>Hall’s Union, 1548.</i>

(4) A pump; a draw-well. <i>Cornw.</i>

(5) A hard blow. <i>Var. dial.</i>

(6) Directly; exactly. <i>Var. dial.</i> Forby has
<i>plumpendicular</i>, perpendicular.

PLUM-PORRIDGE. Porridge with plums in
it, a favourite dish at Christmas in some parts
of the country. It is mentioned as part of
Christmas fare in the Humourist, ed. 1724,
p. 22, and by Addison.

PLUMP-PATE. A thick-headed fellow.

PLUMPY. To churn. <i>Cornw.</i>

PLUMTEN. Plunged. <i>Weber.</i>

PLUM-TREE. The female pudendum. <i>Have</i>
<i>at the plum tree</i> seems to have been either the
burden of a song or a proverbial phrase. It
occurs in Middleton, although Mr. Dyce does
not seem to be acquainted with the meaning
of the term itself, which may be gathered
from Cotgrave, in v. <i>Hoche-prunier</i>, and the
<i>Mariage</i> of Witt and Wisdom, p. 16.

PLUNGE. (1) A deep pool. <i>Somerset.</i>

(2) A strait or difficulty. <i>Greene.</i>

PLUNGY. Wct; rainy. (<i>A.-N.</i>)

PLUNKET. A coarse woollen cloth.

PLUNKY. Short; thick; heavy. <i>East.</i>

PLUNT. A walking-stick, generally one which
has a large knob. <i>Glouc.</i>

PLURISY. Superabundance. <i>Shak.</i>

PLUSHES. The thin hoops which hold a besom
together. <i>West.</i>

PLY. To bend; to consent, or comply. Still
in use in Dorset, Barnes’s Gl.

PLYER. A very common bawd.

PLYMOUTH-CLOAK. A cane, or stick. So
called, says Ray, “because we use a staff <i>in</i>
<i>cuerpo</i>, but not when we wear a cloak.”

PO. A peacock. (<i>A.-S.</i>)
A pruest proud ase a <i>po</i>,
Seththe weddeth us bo.
<i>Wright’s Political Songs, p. 159.</i>

POACHED. Land is said to be <i>poached</i> when it
is trodden with holes by heavy cattle. <i>Var.dia.</i>

POACHING. Swampy. <i>Devon.</i>

POAD-MILK. The first milk given by cows
after calving. <i>Sussex.</i>

POARE-BLIND. Dim-sighted. The word
occurs in Hollyband’s Dictionarie, 1593.

POAT. To kick. <i>Devon.</i>

POBS. Porridge. <i>Craven.</i>

POCHE. A pocket. (<i>A.-N.</i>)
Unto another she dyde as moche;
For they love none but for theyr <i>poche</i>.
<i>The Complaynte of them that ben to late Maryed.</i>

POCHEE. A dish in ancient cookery consisting
principally of poached eggs. <i>Pegge.</i>

POCHERS. Potters?

POCHIN. A hedgehog. <i>Somerset.</i>

POCHIT. A pollard tree. <i>Linc.</i>
